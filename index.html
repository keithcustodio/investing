The philosophy. This is quality-compounder investing in the Terry Smith / Chuck Akre / François Rochon tradition. Long-run returns come not from buying cheap and selling dear, but from owning businesses that earn high returns on incremental capital and can redeploy their own cash flow back into the business at those same high rates for years. A company earning 30% on capital that reinvests most of its earnings at 30% is a compounding machine; over a 10-year hold the multiple you pay matters far less than whether that reinvestment runway is real and durable. The whole system answers two questions: how much owner cash does this business generate, and can it keep compounding that cash at a high rate without competitors arbitraging the return away.

The toll-booth criterion is deliberately narrow. "Wide moat" is too loose, so the universe is restricted to one moat shape: businesses sitting on a chokepoint where activity must flow through them, and where the asset is uncopyable on a ~10-year horizon. The test is mandatory-payment, not preference — index providers, ratings agencies, exchange/data oligopolies, mission-critical financial plumbing. This explicitly excludes brand moats (Colgate, Diageo, Clorox): real, even wide, but demand-preference moats, not tolls. That's why a third of the names that pass a normal "wide moat" screen are cut here and tagged "Not a toll."

The macro is load-bearing — "grinding financial repression." Every valuation assumes structurally elevated rates (US debt & deficit dynamics), with AI productivity as the main offsetting force. The consequence most people miss: high discount rates mean multiple expansion is off the table. Your return cannot come from a re-rating — the market paying 25× next year for what it pays 20× for today. It must come from owner-earnings yield plus reinvestment-driven growth. Any thesis that secretly depends on a re-rate is treated as fragile and flagged — several cut names are rejected precisely because the bull case quietly needs the multiple to expand back to its historical average, which this regime disallows.

Owner earnings, normalized — not reported EPS. The valuation input strips one-time items, separates maintenance capex (required to stand still) from growth capex (discretionary reinvestment), and charges the true economic cost of stock-based comp. Dividing normalized owner-earnings-per-share by the live price gives the owner-earnings yield. This is where reported numbers get challenged, not passed through — e.g. FCF flattered by a working-capital release, or "adjusted" earnings that exclude the same "one-time" charge five years running.

The expected-return engine, and why it's live. Expected annual return = owner-earnings yield + normalized growth. The yield term recomputes off the live price every time the page opens, so a cheaper price mechanically raises expected return and the name climbs. The growth term is held to each deep-dive's conclusion, so the live number stays honest to the research instead of drifting on noise. Because this is the whole return — no re-rating assumed — most great businesses screen as too expensive most of the time: wonderful, but fully priced. The list is mostly a queue waiting for price, and the interesting names are simply the ones with the highest expected return that price hasn't yet competed away.

Risk is structural, not a blended number. Expected return is deliberately not risk-adjusted into one haircut. Risk is a multi-layer gate: a binary quality gate (pass / capped if the moat is narrowing / disqualified if there's no real moat), the 1–100 moat-durability score, and a factor-concentration check that flags clustering in the same exposure. Collapsing quality, durability and correlation into one number destroys information — you want to see cheap-but-eroding versus cheap-and-fortress.

The AI lens runs through everything. For each name: does AI widen or erode this specific moat? For data/network businesses, does the proprietary dataset become more valuable as AI feedstock, or does AI commoditize it? This kills a few otherwise-cheap names (PayPal's checkout toll being disintermediated; NICE billing per human agent-seat in a world where AI deflects the calls) and strengthens others.

Sources are read against their strengths. Morningstar for moat & fair value; Value Line for the historical record and a 3–5yr anchor; BofA for consensus (the thing to disagree with); CFRA for earnings quality. Where a source opines outside its competence it's discounted — e.g. catching that a Morningstar "40% discount to fair value" is mostly an artifact of their low WACC assumption, which largely evaporates under this system's higher discount rate.

Reading the board. Three tabs. Toll Booth & Moat is the shortlist — the names that clear the quality gate cleanly, ranked by expected return. Full universe is all 49 (the 37-name universe plus the 12 cut) in one ranking, with a tag denoting why any name falls short: a grey moat narrowing / no moat tag for in-universe caveats, or a colored badge (weak moat / not a toll / other) for names cut from the universe — hover any tag for the specifics. Factor check shows concentration. Each name shows live price, the 1–100 moat score, moat direction (widening / stable / narrowing), expected return, financial strength (my conclusion — Fortress / Strong / Adequate / Stretched / Weak), capital use (reinvests vs returns cash), a 1–100 conviction score (how robust the thesis is — the probability the return estimate holds), the crux, the next catalyst, and when it was last updated. Click the ⓘ on a header for what a column means; click any ticker (included or cut) for the full thesis card: toll mechanism, AI direction, normalized owner-earnings math, the variant perception (where the market is wrong, and whether there's a genuine edge), the strongest bear case, and the 2–3 quarterly monitors that would signal the thesis breaking. Nothing is asserted without its kill-criteria attached.

A personal research tool, not investment advice. Live quotes are best-effort — useful for ranking, not for placing an order.
